# Farsight

Paste each section into Devpost as-is. Public repo: https://github.com/azizu06/farsight

## Tagline

An iPhone on the rover builds a live 3D depth map. You walk through it in a Quest, over cellular, with no venue Wi-Fi.

## Inspiration

A firefighter, inspector, or rescuer walks into a damaged building on a guess. Scout drones help, then dump a flat phone video on the person who already has both hands full. The useful part (who is in there, where, and whether they are moving) stays a squint.

We built the opposite. The rover's iPhone builds the room in 3D. The operator puts on a Quest and walks through that room. The rover stops itself the moment the link drops. The scout drone in Rainbow Six Siege was the picture in our heads. Training for a real search was the reason to build it.

## What it does

Farsight is a ground rover you inhabit from a Meta Quest 2. No venue Wi-Fi. Two phones carry a Tailscale tunnel on cellular. The rover's iPhone builds the map. The pose, the speech, and the vision run on the rover.

**Stand in the room.** The rover's iPhone 17 Pro LiDAR builds a live 3D map and the Jetson holds it: about 23,000 points and 143,000 triangles, and a later run near 40,000 points, tracking normal. On the captain's live AUTO run the operator Mac kept a measured map of 652,960 points (plus 82,932 on the Jetson) and drew about 557,000 points and 12,500 triangles of it: people, desks and walls you can walk around. With native camera surfaces composited into the live render, the Mac's stereo view then drew 51,026 to 54,430 triangles per frame from the live phone feed, four times as many, at about 28 encoded frames per second. The Mac rebuilds that cloud into a live mesh every 0.5 to 1.4 s: each 4 cm cell with at least 3 samples becomes a vertex, and neighbours only join into a face when their planes and normals agree, so a surface never bridges a depth edge. The Jetson throws out flying pixels at depth edges and grazing angles before they ever leave the rover. Come back to a spot and the map refreshes just the region the camera sees now: a chair that was moved or a person who left is cleared from that view, while walls behind it and rooms you can't currently see stay exactly as measured. In the Quest you stand inside that map. The camera fills the whole measured frame at its own aspect ratio (the old 70% crop is gone), and the screen stays level and anchored from the moment you enter, so you can look around it naturally. The headset shows almost nothing else: one MANUAL / AUTONOMOUS pill and a small A hint. Telemetry, stop causes and the advanced controls live on the laptop page, so the view stays the room.

**Drive.** The headset starts in MANUAL. Push the right thumbstick and the rover drives and turns; center it and the rover stops. No trigger to hold. Full stick asks for the full configured motor output with no jerk at the first push: forward eases to 90% over 0.3 s and on to full over about 2 s, while letting go still stops at once. Steering keeps your forward speed, so a diagonal push carves a real arc instead of collapsing into a spin. Steering is shaped so the rover only turns on purpose: a push within 20° of straight drives dead straight, a 0.25 dead zone ignores thumb wobble, and an expo curve gives fine control near center and a full pivot at the edge. The Jetson issues a 250 ms permit at 40 Hz and the headset spends each one the instant it arrives, so a dropped link lets the lease run out and the motors stop. B in the headset, STOP on the desktop, and the stop button on the Uno each stop the rover at once, and after a hard stop the stick has to come back to center before it drives again. The Arduino Uno zeros the motors on its own after 300 ms without a command. In the demo build a camera hiccup shows a VIDEO STALE warning instead of stopping hand driving, and a brief controller blip of up to 1 s is ridden out instead of killing the drive. On the real rig, wheels up, driving on that iPhone LiDAR feed sent 74 nonzero-PWM statuses at v=0.4, and 0 after release.

**Let it roam, walk the map.** Press A and the rover switches to AUTONOMOUS: it explores on its own, hands-free. Explore never ends on its own. When the room has no unexplored edge left, it picks an open destination in the mapped room and commits to a route around whatever is in the way, instead of standing still, and it drives out of tight wall pockets rather than turning in place. It stops for B, a dropped control link, the Uno, or a sensor stall longer than 2 s, and it never waits on the headset's picture. A sensor hiccup under 2 s holds the motors at zero and the same Explore drives straight on by itself, with no re-press and no lost goal, and a late control status from a brief server stall keeps Explore armed instead of dropping it. It runs at full power where it is open: with nothing measured within 0.8 m it drives at the rover's top speed (PWM 255, about 0.30 m/s fully loaded), easing down to a 0.15 m/s floor as walls close in to 0.3 m, and it turns in place at 2.2 rad/s. In simulation that Explore covers 90% of the reachable floor in 118.2 s with 0 collisions. The 3D map opens around you at 1:1 scale and keeps updating while the rover drives. The left stick walks and strafes you through it, the right stick moves you forward and back and a sideways flick snap-turns 30°. Walking the map never moves the rover. Add `?macview=1` and the walk-through becomes the map rendered on the operator Mac's GPU, streamed in as H.264; `?macview=1&stereo=1` asks for a separate picture for each eye. Or open `/explore.html` and step out of the driving seat entirely: a dedicated walkthrough of the reconstructed room, with head-relative walking and strafing, 30° snap turns, moving up and down, and a home reset. The Mac does all the fusing, meshing, lighting and per-eye rendering and streams it as H.264, so the headset downloads no geometry and opens no connection to the rover's controls. B stops everything and lands you back in MANUAL. Press A again for MANUAL, and the autonomy hands back to you.

**Find people.** YOLO11n-pose on the Jetson Orin Nano, a TensorRT FP16 engine, boxes every person, tracks them with stable ids, and flags someone who appears to be lying down. On the board, raw inference runs at a median 15.29 ms and p95 18.83 ms (20 zero-input runs) and found 4 people in a real public photo. On the laptop page, click a person and the rover Seeks them. The planner treats people as solid: each detected person becomes a 0.3 m body footprint, and anyone within 1.2 m of another is wrapped into one crowd shape, so the rover never routes through the floor between someone's legs or squeezes through a small group. It goes around, and separate groups still leave the aisle between them open.

**Ask the rover.** Ask from the laptop page, by voice or by typing. Whisper base.en transcribes on the Jetson GPU. Qwen3-VL-2B answers from the picture and the detections on that same GPU. Piper speaks the answer, and plays preset survivor phrases in English or Spanish. On the Orin Nano, Qwen3-VL-2B uses 2.77 GB and reaches a first token in 0.24 to 0.78 s. Whisper base.en transcribes a short clip in 0.24 s warm. End to end (speech, vision, voice) lands in 2.0 to 3.4 s. The assistant proposes Seek or Scan. It never moves the motors on its own.

**Scan the room.** The rover stops, looks in five directions, and Depth Anything V2 turns each view into metric depth. You stand inside the point cloud in VR. A LiDAR iPhone supplies measured depth instead, and the viewer says which one you are looking at. A five-view scan measured on a Mac takes 596.5 ms and produces 89,967 points.

**Walk the room after the run.** While the rover drives, the Jetson quietly archives the phone's full camera frames with their ARKit poses and lens intrinsics, up to 2.5 per second, off the control path, and saves the measured LiDAR points beside each photo so a run's scan survives even a Jetson reboot. After the run the operator Mac pulls that capture and trains a photoreal Gaussian splat of the room on its own GPU (Brush on Metal), under a fixed time budget, then trims it to at most 300,000 splats so a Quest can hold it. Put the headset back on and you walk through the room the rover just saw, in stereo: left stick to walk where you look, right stick to snap-turn. The viewer loads public sample scenes of 175,745 to 1,026,508 splats in 105 to 2,063 ms on a desktop check.

**Plan the path.** Nav2 plans go-to, Explore, and Seek. Double-click the floor of the laptop's map for a go-to goal, and the map draws the path, the goal, and the frontier markers. In five simulated worlds, across every closed-loop navigation scenario, 0 collisions. With a box appearing in the corridor, the simulated rover reaches the goal in 26.4 s, longest standstill 0.41 s. Explore covers 91% of a three-room floor in 25.0 s. Seek follows a walking person and holds a 1.18 m median gap behind a 0.8 m target. In the same simulated rooms, SLAM Toolbox cuts ARKit-like drift on a 16.7 m loop from 0.34 m to 0.06 m, and heavy drift from 1.36 m to 0.03 m. An optional nvblox path can build that costmap on the GPU: depth integrated into a TSDF, sliced into a 2D obstacle map.

**Run the drill. Leave the record.** A 90-second rescue drill scores the search: a face held in view for one second counts, emergency stops cost you, and you get a score plus a route home. Every encounter lands in a mission report with time, snapshot, and an SBAR triage note drafted on the rover. A pose that changes from standing to lying writes the note again. Runs record and replay.

Over two real phones, Wi-Fi off, control round-trip sits at p50 119.5 ms, p95 167 ms, max 228 ms. Expired permits: 0 in 30 seconds. H.264 holds about 9 fps on that same cellular chain.

The controllers, in one place. Two modes, three buttons. MANUAL: the right thumbstick drives, center it to stop. A switches MANUAL and AUTONOMOUS on its press. In AUTONOMOUS the sticks walk you through the map and never move the rover. B stops, cancels navigation, and returns to MANUAL. On the laptop: hold Space and use WASD to drive, click a person to Seek, E explores, double-click the map floor for go-to, C cancels, and STOP stops.

## How we built it

Two kits. One brain. The map is built where the camera is.

The operator kit is a Quest 2, a MacBook, and an iPhone 16 Pro. The MacBook runs the operator relay: it serves the headset app on 127.0.0.1:8080 and proxies every HTTP and WebSocket route to the rover. `adb reverse` carries that port to the Quest over USB, so Quest Browser gets a local secure origin and enters WebXR. TypeScript, Three.js, and Vite render the video texture, decode H.264, match boxes to frames, and draw the ray cursor, the level view, the 1:1 map walk-through, and a minimal HUD.

The rover kit is a Jetson Orin Nano on an Anker power bank, an iPhone 17 Pro mounted fixed as the camera and the LiDAR, and an ELEGOO Arduino Uno on the motors. The phone tethers to the Jetson over USB. That gives a local camera link and a cellular uplink. The main path is phone-to-phone Tailscale over cellular: the Mac and the Jetson stay on USB, and the computers do not need Tailscale. The bridge app binds its listener once and answers HTTP 200 from the Jetson over the tailnet. An optional direct path to the Jetson's own tailnet address is the fallback, off by default. Each new connection tries the phone path first and takes the direct path if the phone path does not answer.

The map takes its own path. The rover iPhone builds the mesh natively in ARKit, then sends geometry deltas (a 124-byte header plus vertices and triangles, compressed with native Brotli) to the operator Mac. The Mac assembles the latest replacement for each anchor and serves the headset its map snapshots locally, in Rust (`farsight-geometry`). The Jetson keeps the work that has to stay next to the motors: inference, the safety lease, and the measured depth and pose that Nav2 reads. `--fusion-bypass` lets it skip building a second visual map when the phone lane is carrying the geometry. Measured LiDAR and estimated depth stay labelled all the way to the headset.

The Mac can take the rendering off the headset too. With `?macview=1`, the operator Mac renders the 3D map on its own GPU, hardware-encodes it with VideoToolbox (`h264_videotoolbox`), and streams it to the Quest in AUTO. The headset sends its pose back, so the Mac camera follows both your head and the stick. The dense cloud holds up to 5 million bounded measured cells, drawn as lit splats with an ACES colour grade. `?stereo=1` asks for a side-by-side 1920x1080 pair rendered per eye, and the Quest shows each half only to its own eye. If Mac frames stop for a second, the headset's own map comes back.

On the Jetson, one FastAPI process owns the live stack:

- Camera frames arrive from our iOS capture app: RGB, or LiDAR RGB-D with ARKit pose. A missing camera stays missing.
- FFmpeg encodes fragmented-MP4 H.264 and tags every sample with its capture id.
- Perception runs YOLO11n-pose through TensorRT FP16, an IoU-plus-velocity tracker, and an optional UltraFace pass for the drill. A TensorRT failure rebuilds the engine, at most once every 5 seconds.
- Control owns safety: a 250 ms permit lease, a 500 ms stale-view guard on Explore, Seek, and go-to (the demo build lifts it, so hand driving and autonomy run on the rover's own sensors), and the autonomy modes themselves. The Uno independently zeros the motors after 300 ms, on an invalid command, an over-long line, or the D2 button. The sketch is 5100 bytes of flash and 292 bytes of RAM.
- Mapping fuses live LiDAR on the board, and bypasses that fusion when the phone geometry lane is the source of the mesh.
- The mission log, the drill scorer, and triage write the report. Whisper base.en and Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct (Q4_K_M, 1.55 GB, through a CUDA llama.cpp) run on the board's GPU. Piper speaks on the board's CPU.

Nav2 (ROS 2 Jazzy) plans on a loopback bridge. `cmd_vel` reaches the rover only while the control heartbeat carries a live deadman and a valid permit, and the phone session still has a normal-tracking ARKit pose (at most 1 s old) and LiDAR depth (at most 1 s old). In the demo build a stall shorter than 2 s holds the motors at zero and drives on once the sensors are fresh; a longer one stops. No dead reckoning. A go-to goal posted while the rover is stopped is staged: it sits armed, and the next fresh deadman press is what runs it. The local costmap is a 5 x 5 m window at 5 cm, updated at 15 Hz, so a pop-up obstacle lands on the next cycle. Regulated Pure Pursuit runs at 20 Hz. SLAM Toolbox publishes the drift correction as `map -> odom`. Swap `costmap_source` to nvblox and the obstacle layer can read a GPU ESDF slice instead of the raw depth cloud.

A fault proxy and link-budget harness run the real server, real FFmpeg, and real pose inference behind a bandwidth cap. Raw map points, not video, saturated a 2 Mbps link. The viewer pauses map transfers while you drive and pulls compressed snapshots when you stop. After the 40 Hz permit change, held-drive video holds 10.0 fps on a direct profile (8/4 Mbps) and a DERP-typical profile (6/3 Mbps). Under an emulated 2 Mbps cap, with the map paused, the proxy sits at about 635 kbps while driving.

A byte-compatible Rust port of the server and the relay speaks the same wire protocol. The Quest app and the iPhone app do not change.

1,091 Python tests (plus 276 subtests) cover control state, tracking, pose decode, drill, scan, navigation, and the assistant. The Quest app has 273 unit tests and the Rust port 225. A 15-step judge flow drives the whole path in a real browser: page load, drive, Explore, Seek, drill, Ask, report, scan, nav view, replay.

## Challenges we ran into

**A deadman that survives a bad network.** A deadman over cellular is only as good as its worst pause. Early runs expired the 250 ms lease during short stalls. We kept the 250 ms and fixed the causes: status-triggered command sends, a bounded CPU thread pool for pose, and camera capture decoupled from map fusion. Emulating a Tailscale DERP leg still expired the lease in ordinary relay jitter. The headset now echoes a permit the moment it arrives, and the Jetson issues permits at 40 Hz instead of 20 Hz. The lease stayed 250 ms. The stutter threshold moved from about 125 ms to about 155 ms of network round-trip p95. On the real two-phone chain, expired permits went from 4 per 30 seconds to 0.

**A view cropped to 70%.** The first VR build cropped the phone frame to 70% by 70% and let the operator look around inside the leftover. It hid the edges of the room the LiDAR had already measured. The headset now shows the full measured frame, fitted to its real aspect ratio, anchored level when you enter, so looking around the room never drags the screen with your head.

**Video that kept seeking.** The viewer's H.264 player kept jumping to the live edge to catch up, so the browser spent most of its time seeking and playback sat at 4.24 Hz. Now it trims lag by playing slightly fast and seeks only for a real backlog. The same player carries the rover camera and the Mac view. On a native Metal desktop check it plays at 29.35 Hz with zero seeks.

**Boxes on the wrong frame.** With the detector slower than the camera, exact frame matching drew boxes on about one frame in five. The viewer now takes the newest same-source result within eight frames and fades it by its lag.

**Bandwidth was the map.** The 3D map alone needed more than a 2 Mbps link. The fix was scheduling, not a worse picture. Under an emulated 2 Mbps cap, held-drive video receipt p95 fell from about 1.1 s to about 310 ms at a 100 ms round trip, and moving-frame delivery went from 0% to 100%.

**Bringing up the Jetson.** The board needed USB recovery before it would boot. NVIDIA's FFmpeg build ships without libx264, so we installed a private software encoder beside it. The CH340 serial driver was missing from the kernel, so we built it out of tree and flashed the Uno over `/dev/ttyUSB0`.

**Honest depth.** A depth model draws a convincing point cloud whether or not it is right. Estimated and measured geometry stay labelled separately, all the way to the headset. Building the mesh on the phone keeps that split clean: the iPhone sends what ARKit measured, and the Jetson never has to guess the room in order to drive it.

**A phone bridge that would not start.** Tapping Start bridge failed on both phones with `POSIX(22)` because the bind port was set twice. Binding only through the local endpoint fixed it. The rover listener then answered HTTP 200 from the Jetson over Tailscale.

**A planner that dithered.** Inflation at 0.35 m made the rover hesitate in a 0.65 m gap. A 0.13 m radius let it clip a wall end. Robot radius 0.16 m and inflation 0.28 m keep a zero-cost centre line through that gap. For the live demo we tightened it to radius 0.17 m and inflation 0.22 m, the footprint plus 5 cm, so the rover threads gaps just over its own width. Differencing single 30 Hz poses made MPPI believe the rover was still spinning, so it spun in place. Odometry twist is now estimated over a 150 ms window. In simulation on the earlier 0.16 / 0.28 m setup, RPP cleared a pop-up obstacle without a stop; MPPI stood still 1.22 s on the same world.

## What we learned

Put safety on the robot. The headset and the network fail first, so the rover stops by itself when they do. Three independent timers (the 250 ms permit, the 500 ms stale view, the 300 ms Uno watchdog) beat one clever timer.

Measure before tuning. The bandwidth problem was the map, not the video. A lease failure was a stalled thread pool, not a timeout that needed stretching. We kept 250 ms and fixed the cause.

Build the mesh where the camera is. The phone already has the LiDAR and the pose. Shipping compressed geometry deltas to the operator and leaving the Jetson on inference, control, and navigation depth keeps each machine on the job it is good at.

Label everything. Simulated, replay, estimated, and measured stay visible in the headset. Cheap to draw. Expensive to skip. It stopped us from fooling ourselves in rehearsal.

A planner's radii are driving decisions, not config defaults. Dropping inflation 7 cm (0.35 m to 0.28 m) stopped the dither in a 0.65 m gap. Raising the robot radius 3 cm (0.13 m to 0.16 m) stopped the wall clip. Together they hold a zero-cost centre line through that gap.

## What's next for Farsight

Floor the calibration. A drive profile turns Nav2's metres per second into the PWM this chassis actually answers, then we raise speed only after a clean run.

A second rover, and handing control between operators, on the same permit, the same stop, the same report.

Give the assistant memory of where people were, so "what did you see in the last room" has an answer.

Train the first splat from a full rover run and walk it in the headset, now that capture, training and the viewer are all in place.

Keep SLAM on for any run that loops back. On a short straight demo ARKit is enough. On a revisit, the 0.06 m correction is the map.
